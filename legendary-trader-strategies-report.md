## How to read the evidence

Every number below carries a confidence tag:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[V]** | Verified against a primary or near-primary source I opened this session (e.g., Berkshire's own letter, an NBER/SSRN paper). |
| **[R]** | Reported by reputable press or academics, but the underlying track record is private, reconstructed, or I only saw it second-hand. Treat as approximate. |
| **[W]** | Widely repeated but weakly sourced (blogs, aggregators, books). Treat as folklore until confirmed. |
| **[N/A]** | Not publicly available. |

Hedge-fund returns are almost never audited publicly. Most "legendary" numbers come from investor letters leaked to the press, books, or the manager's own marketing. Only Berkshire (public company, audited) has a fully verifiable record. That alone is a finding: **the better the headline number, the less verifiable it tends to be.**

## 1. Strategy breakdown

### Warren Buffett: concentrated value / quality compounding
- **Strategy:** Buy durable businesses with pricing power at reasonable prices, hold for decades. Berkshire also owns whole companies (insurance, railroads, utilities) and uses insurance "float" as cheap leverage.
- **In practice:** Few positions, huge size. Waits for fat pitches; holds cash when nothing is cheap (cash pile is a feature).
- **Markets:** US equities mostly; private companies; insurance. Almost never shorts or derivatives for profit.
- **Risk management:** Doesn't use stop-losses. Risk = permanent loss of capital, controlled by margin of safety, business quality, no forced selling (no redemptions, float is sticky). Position sizing: top 5 holdings often >60-70% of the equity portfolio [R].
- **Inputs:** Financial statements, owner earnings, ROIC, moat, management quality. No technical indicators.
- **Horizon:** Very long (decades).

### George Soros: reflexivity-driven global macro
- **Strategy:** Spot self-reinforcing feedback loops (boom/bust) between prices and fundamentals, e.g., currency regimes that policy cannot defend. Bet big when there is an identifiable policy mistake.
- **In practice:** Starts with a thesis, tests it with a small position, then scales hard if the market confirms. Famous 1992 short of sterling.
- **Markets:** Currencies, rates, equities, commodities, globally; futures and FX with leverage.
- **Risk management:** "Survive first." Size up only when conviction and market feedback align; exits fast when the thesis breaks. Heavy leverage on high-conviction trades.
- **Inputs:** Macro/policy analysis, central bank behavior, positioning, news flow.
- **Horizon:** Weeks to a couple of years.
- Note: Stanley Druckenmiller ran Quantum day to day from 1988 to 2000, so a large share of the 1990s' fame belongs to him [R].

### Jim Simons / Renaissance (Medallion): statistical, high-frequency quant
- **Strategy:** Thousands of small statistical edges (mean reversion, short-term patterns, cross-asset signals) discovered with massive data and signal-processing/ML, executed with strict risk controls and low market exposure.
- **In practice:** Very high number of trades, very short holding periods, tiny edge per trade (win rate reportedly just above 50%) repeated millions of times [R].
- **Markets:** Futures, equities, FX, rates, liquid instruments globally.
- **Risk management:** Highly diversified, market-neutral-ish (Cornell estimates beta about -1.0 vs the market [R]), leverage reportedly high, capacity strictly capped (~$10B, employees only) [R].
- **Inputs:** Proprietary data, models, execution infrastructure, PhDs in math/physics/CS.
- **Horizon:** Very short (seconds to days).

### Ray Dalio / Bridgewater: systematic global macro + risk parity
- **Strategy:** Two products. **Pure Alpha:** systematic and discretionary macro bets across ~100+ markets. **All Weather:** risk-balanced portfolio designed to do OK across growth/inflation regimes (levered bonds, equities, commodities, inflation-linked).
- **Risk management:** Diversification across uncorrelated bets (the "Holy Grail" idea of 15+ uncorrelated return streams), risk parity weighting, leverage to equalize risk contribution.
- **Inputs:** Economic "machine" models: debt cycles, growth, inflation, central bank liquidity; decades of data.
- **Horizon:** Medium to long (months to years).

### Paul Tudor Jones: discretionary global macro + technical timing
- **Strategy:** Directional bets on equity indices, rates, currencies, commodities. Uses technicals (e.g., 200-day moving average) and historical analogs (his famous 1987 crash call used a 1929 chart analog).
- **Risk management:** "Defense first." Cut losers fast, never average down, risk small per idea, then press winners. Rule of thumb he promotes: "play great defense."
- **Markets:** Futures and FX mainly.
- **Horizon:** Days to months.

### Stanley Druckenmiller: concentrated macro + liquidity
- **Strategy:** Follow central bank liquidity and the direction of the macro "earnings and liquidity" cycle; invest big in a few high-conviction positions, then pivot hard when the picture changes.
- **Risk management:** Famous rule: "It's not whether you're right or wrong, but how much you make when you're right." Concentrates when conviction is high; ruthless about cutting.
- **Markets:** Equities, rates, currencies, commodities.
- **Horizon:** Months to a couple of years.

### Steve Cohen / SAC → Point72: high-turnover discretionary equity + multi-manager
- **Strategy:** SAC was a high-turnover long/short equity operation built on rapid information processing and trading around catalysts. Point72 is a multi-manager platform (many PM teams) with strict stop-outs, plus a systematic arm.
- **Risk management:** Hard loss limits per PM/trader (reported ~5% drawdown triggers cuts [R]), diversification across many managers, central risk oversight.
- **Horizon:** Short (days to weeks) per PM.
- **Important caveat:** SAC Capital pleaded guilty to insider trading in 2013 and paid about $1.8B in penalties, and Cohen was barred from managing outside money for two years. Part of SAC's historical edge may have been illegal, so its track record is not a model to copy.

### Notes for fair comparison

1. **Medallion's average is arithmetic and gross/net is for a fund no outsider can invest in.** Cornell's own calculation puts its Sharpe ratio above 2 [R]. Treat it as the best *reported* strategy, not a benchmark.
2. **Size matters.** Medallion stays near $10B by design. Buffett compounded at 19.9% while managing hundreds of billions late in the record, so his *dollar* profits are far larger than his percentage return suggests.
3. **Leverage.** Buffett's alpha study found Berkshire's Sharpe ratio of 0.76 beats every stock or mutual fund with a 30+ year history, and its returns are explained by buying cheap, safe, high-quality stocks plus roughly 1.6x leverage via insurance float [V: Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, NBER w19681].
4. **Fees.** Hedge fund returns are quoted gross or net inconsistently. Medallion's gross-to-net gap (66% to 39%) reflects its famously high fees (5% management, 44% performance).
5. **Decay.** Berkshire's outperformance was concentrated before 2003; since then it has been much closer to the index (consistent with Alaric Securities analysis [R]; the 2024 letter table confirms the late-record margins are far narrower). Macro funds (Tudor, Soros) also saw returns fall sharply after the 2000s.

## 4. Which strategies actually made the most money

**Highest percentage returns:** Medallion by a wide margin (39% net average for 30 years is unmatched among reported records), then Soros/Quantum, Druckenmiller, and SAC in the ~30% range.

**Highest total dollar profits:** Buffett/Berkshire (decades of compounding at enormous scale) and Bridgewater (reported as the most profitable hedge fund in dollars) lead. Medallion's author-estimated lifetime trading profits are on the order of $100B [W: Zuckerman], but treat that as an estimate.

**Best risk-adjusted:** Medallion (Sharpe above 2 per Cornell [R]), then Thorp-style statistical arbitrage. Among *verifiable* records, Berkshire's Sharpe of 0.76 beats every stock or fund with a 30+ year history [V], which is remarkable for a strategy anyone can understand.

**Most consistent over the long term:** Berkshire (verified, 60 years, though 11 negative years and drawdowns up to -48.7%). Bridgewater's Pure Alpha has a long record but with modest ~11% annualized.

**Most sustainable:** In order of durability:
1. **Value/quality compounding**: edge rooted in behavior (patience), not in speed or secrecy, so it cannot be arbitraged away entirely.
2. **Diversified systematic strategies** (trend following, risk parity, stat arb): edge comes from many small bets, but it requires infrastructure.
3. **Discretionary macro**: depends on individual genius; returns decayed after the founders' peak years.

**Caution on "headline" returns:** Medallion's 66% is gross of enormous fees and for a capacity-capped fund. Druckenmiller and Soros are reported, not audited. SAC's record is clouded by the insider trading case. Only Berkshire is fully verified.

## 5. Top three strategies a retail trader can realistically learn

The evidence on retail trading is sobering and needs to frame everything:

- **Day trading:** A Brazilian study of every individual who day-traded equity-index futures (started 2013-2015 and persisted 300+ days) found **97% lost money, and only 0.4% earned more than a bank teller**, with no evidence of learning over time [R: Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti, "Day Trading for a Living?"; later versions report different cut-offs, so the exact percentages vary but the conclusion is the same].
- **Frequent trading:** Barber & Odean's classic study of ~66,000 households found the most active traders earned **11.4% annually vs 17.9% for the market**, with the most active fifth earning 5.5 percentage points less than the least active, largely because of costs and overconfidence [V: *Journal of Finance*, 2000].

Given that, the three most realistic strategies for you are low-turnover ones:

### #1: Quality / value investing with a long horizon (Buffett-Lynch style)
- **Skills:** Reading financial statements, valuing businesses, patience, emotional discipline. Books: *The Intelligent Investor*, Buffett's shareholder letters, *One Up on Wall Street*.
- **Timeframe:** 3-10+ years.
- **Markets:** Stocks (and broad index funds as the core). Indices as default. Not suited to forex or crypto.
- **Risk management:** 10-25 holdings max, no single position >10-15% at cost, don't use leverage, hold a cash buffer, only buy what you'd be comfortable holding through a 50% drawdown.
- **Starting capital (estimate):** Can start with **$500-$5,000** thanks to fractional shares and low-cost brokers; meaningful compounding needs regular contributions.
- **Learning curve:** Moderate. 6-12 months to be competent, years to be good. Easy to implement, hard to stay disciplined.

### #2: Trend following / time-series momentum (Dennis, PTJ's trend filter, managed futures)
- **Skills:** Rule-based systems, backtesting basics, strict position sizing. Resources: *Following the Trend* (Clenow), *Trend Following* (Covel), AQR's research.
- **Evidence:** AQR's study simulates trend following back to 1880 and finds it profitable across the 110 years studied, i.e., it is not a statistical fluke of recent decades [V: AQR, *A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing*, JPM 2017]. It tends to do well in sustained crises (2008, 2022) and poorly in choppy, range-bound markets.
- **Timeframe:** Weeks to months (daily or weekly bars; no screen-watching).
- **Markets:** Index futures/ETFs, commodities, FX, bond ETFs; crypto can work but is more volatile. Simple versions use a 10-month or 200-day moving average on diversified ETFs.
- **Risk management:** Risk ~0.25-1% of account per trade, volatility-based position sizing (ATR), diversified across 10+ markets, accept many small losses.
- **Starting capital (estimate):** **$2,000-$10,000** using ETFs; **$25,000-$50,000+** for a diversified futures approach (micro contracts reduce this).
- **Learning curve:** Moderate to high. Mechanically simple, psychologically hard (long drawdowns, ~35-45% win rate).

### #3: Diversified, risk-balanced portfolio (Dalio-style All Weather, simplified)
- **Skills:** Asset allocation, rebalancing, understanding correlations. Resources: Dalio's *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises*, risk parity primers.
- **Timeframe:** Multi-year, rebalance annually/quarterly.
- **Markets:** ETFs across stocks, long/intermediate bonds, gold, commodities, inflation-linked bonds. No leverage in the retail version.
- **Risk management:** Fixed allocation weights, rebalance bands, no leverage. Know that 2022 showed stocks and bonds can fall together.
- **Starting capital (estimate):** **$1,000-$10,000**.
- **Learning curve:** Low. Lowest effort, lowest skill requirement, and a sensible core for a student.

**Honest note:** These are in order of my confidence they'll help you *build wealth*, not order of excitement. Short-term trading (day trading, forex scalping, leveraged crypto) is not on the list because the evidence says the odds are heavily against you.

## 6. Final verdict

| Question | Answer | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Most profitable historically** | By % return: **Medallion (Simons)**. By total dollars: **Buffett/Berkshire** (verified), with Bridgewater close | Medallion [R]; Berkshire [V] |
| **2. Best risk-adjusted** | **Medallion** (Sharpe >2), but unreplicable; best *verifiable* is **Berkshire** (Sharpe 0.76, beats all 30+ year funds) | [R] / [V] |
| **3. Most consistent over time** | **Buffett/Berkshire**: 60 years, 19.9% CAGR vs 10.4% S&P, 49 up years of 60 | [V] |
| **4. Best for a retail trader** | **Low-turnover quality/value investing plus an index core**, optionally a simple trend-following overlay | Evidence-based judgment |
| **5. Greatest potential for long-term wealth creation** | **Buffett-style compounding**: modest edge, long time, no leverage, low costs. The math of 15-20% over decades dwarfs most trading ideas, and it is the one strategy anyone can actually run | Judgment, supported by [V] data |

### Key takeaways for you
1. The biggest returns came from **edges retail traders cannot copy** (Medallion's data and speed, macro funds' leverage and access, SAC's information flow).
2. The most **replicable** edge is also the oldest: buy good businesses cheaply, hold them, keep costs and taxes low, and let compounding work. Time is your biggest advantage as a student.
3. **Edge decays.** Berkshire's alpha mostly came before 2003, and macro funds' returns fell after the 2000s. No strategy is permanent.
4. Treat any claim of "30%+ per year" with suspicion unless audited. Survivorship bias is huge: we study the winners.
5. Practical path: learn on a **paper account** or with small money; start with a diversified core; add skills (valuation, trend rules) gradually; track every decision in a journal.
